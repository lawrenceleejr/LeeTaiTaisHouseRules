Chapters 1–6 taught you how the game is played here. This chapter is the
fine print: the specific provisions that decide arguments, plus the table
law that isn't about tiles at all. If you learned elsewhere, some of these
will scandalize you. Sit with that feeling. Then comply.

## The gameplay articles

{{< houserule title="Article 1 — Fourteen dead tiles" >}}
The last **14 tiles of the wall are dead** and are never drawn. Wall reaches
the dead zone with no winner? Wash ({{< zh "流局" "lau guk" >}}), no
payments, re-deal.
{{< /houserule >}}

{{< houserule title="Article 2 — The point minimum is negotiable" >}}
Chicken hands are worth 0 points, legal by default, and pay the base stake.
A **point minimum may be applied** — but it's declared before the first
deal, like a civilized threat, not after somebody wins with garbage.
{{< /houserule >}}

{{< houserule title="Article 3 — The payment formula" >}}
**Per person: chicken × 2^(points).** Discarder pays double. Zi-Mo: everyone
pays double. Capped at **6 points — "Spicy"** ({{< zh "辣" "laat" >}}). The
[full math]({{< relref "scoring" >}}) has a worked example and the ladder.
{{< /houserule >}}

{{< houserule title="Article 4 — The 9-cards clause" >}}
When a player's exposed melds show nine tiles of one suit, the table calls
**"9-cards!"** — and any discarder who completes that full flush afterward
**pays all debts**, all three shares, alone. You were warned. Out loud. On
the record.
{{< /houserule >}}

{{< houserule title="Article 5 — Seat priority" >}}
Verbatim, no apologies: **Chinese people get seat priority, in an effort to
preserve the culture.** Guests are honored, welcomed, fed — and seated
second. This is called heritage programming and it comes with snacks.
{{< /houserule >}}

{{< houserule title="Article 6 — Couples sit across" >}}
**Couples should sit across from each other.** Adjacent seating enables
footsie-based information transfer and no one has time to relitigate the
2019 "we weren't signaling" hearings. Across the table, where the table can
see your love *and* your discards.
{{< /houserule >}}

## Table law

The articles above cover the game. These cover the room. Same force of
law — arguably more.

- **Tai-Pong is a request for grace.** Saying
  {{< zh "太碰" "tai-pong" >}} means *hold on, let me think about this one.*
  It claims nothing. The table gives you the beat.
- **Trash talk: encouraged. Coaching: forbidden.** One voice per hand. Your
  cousin's boyfriend does not need three advisors; he needs to lose
  authentically, like everyone before him.
- **A false win ({{< zh "詐糊" "zaa wu" >}}) pays the table.** Declare a win
  you don't have and you pay everyone the Spicy maximum. Check twice.
  Declare once.
- **The wall is sacred.** Touch it out of turn and the whole table will make
  the same sound at you. You'll know the sound when you hear it.
- **First win is on the house.** A first-timer's first-ever win is
  celebrated, photographed, and paid double by the table. Nobody's buying
  beginner tears at this establishment. This is also how we recruit.
- **The peanut gallery eats quietly.** Watching is free; opinions cost. The
  chair behind TaiTai is the best lesson you can get — take it, and take it
  silently.

## Frequently argued questions

**Can we play with jokers?**
No. We're delighted your office plays American mah-jongg — you can play
American rules at someone else's house.

**What do we play for?**
Default: **a penny a chicken.** Before the first deal, the table can discuss
playing for more or less — dimes, chips, dish duty, whatever — and whatever
it agrees on holds for the session. Nobody adjusts it mid-hand because
they're losing.

**What if only three people show up?**
{{< zh "三缺一" "saam kyut yat" "three lacking one" >}} is a state of
emergency, and emergency powers apply: cousins may be woken, neighbors may be
recruited, and yes, the person who "just came to drop off oranges" is now a
mahjong player. The oranges were a contract.

{{< taitai >}}
People ask why we write rules down for a family game. Because rules are how
you argue *warmly*. When it's written, nobody's memory has to win — we just
point at the rule, someone says "fine," and we play the next hand. That's
family, in one sentence.
{{< /taitai >}}
