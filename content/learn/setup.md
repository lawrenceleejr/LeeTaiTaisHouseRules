Setup is where mahjong stops being a game and becomes a ritual. Every step
here has a practical anti-cheating origin and a spiritual auntie overlay, and
you will do all of it every single hand, forever, until your hands know the
choreography without you.

## Seats and winds

The four seats are the four winds: {{< zh "東" "dung" "east" >}},
{{< zh "南" "naam" "south" >}}, {{< zh "西" "sai" "west" >}},
{{< zh "北" "bak" "north" >}} — and they run **counterclockwise**, which will
feel wrong for exactly one evening and then feel like the only sane way to
arrange humans.

To start, someone rolls the dice and sets the **round wind marker** in the
centre of the table. Count that number around the table counterclockwise,
beginning with the thrower as one. Whoever you land on takes the **east**
seat, and east is the first **dealer** ({{< zh "莊" "zong" >}}). Everyone
else falls into south, west and north from there.

East effectively owns the first hand: the dealer wins and loses bigger, and
here the dealer who wins **stays dealer** — cue groaning. Chapter 6 has the
money side.

Winds do double duty:

- **Your seat wind** is yours until the deal rotates past you.
- **The round wind** starts as East for the whole first round — the deal must
  pass through all four players before the round wind moves to South, and so
  on through a full session of four rounds, four
  {{< zh "一圈" "yat hyun" "one circuit" >}} in all. That's the traditional
  unit of play. Budget three to four hours and one meaningful family
  revelation.

## Washing the tiles

All 144 tiles go face-down in the middle and everybody shuffles them in big
circles with both hands. This is called **washing** ({{< zh "洗牌" "sai paai" >}})
and it must be done *loudly*. The racket is the point: it's the game's
doorbell, audible from any room in the house, and it summons the fourth player
the way nothing else can. There is a reason
{{< zh "三缺一" "saam kyut yat" "three lacking one" >}} — "we have three, we
need a fourth" — is one of the most emotionally coercive phrases in the
language.

{{< taitai >}}
Wash like you mean it. Quiet washing is how people get accused of stacking
tiles, and I've seen a Thanksgiving end over less.
{{< /taitai >}}

## Building the wall

Each player builds a wall in front of themselves: **18 stacks long, 2 tiles
high**, face-down. Four walls, pushed together into a square — a little
fortress with all the tiles inside, and yes, 4 × 36 = 144, it all accounts
for itself.

This is the part to show off on. Anyone can push tiles into a line; the craft
is in the **top row** — lining up a full run of tiles and dropping the whole
lot onto the bottom row in one motion. The longer the drop, the higher the
cred. It is completely fine to be a baby about it and do it a chunk at a
time. Everyone starts there.

Mark the **replacement end** while you're building: stack the last stack of
the wall **three or four tiles high** instead of two. That's the visible flag
for where kong replacements and flower patches get drawn from, so nobody has
to ask mid-hand.

## The dice decide everything

The dealer rolls **three dice** — always three — and counts that total
around the table counterclockwise, starting with themselves, to pick whose
wall gets broken. Then they count that same number of stacks in from that
wall's right edge and lift the deal from there.

Is this over-engineered? Deeply. That's the anti-cheating genius of it: nobody
— not even the dealer — can know in advance which part of which wall becomes
whose hand. The dice launder the luck. Respect the dice.

## The deal

From the break point, going counterclockwise:

1. Each player takes **4 tiles at a time** — two stacks — until everyone has
   12\. Dealer first, obviously.
2. Then each player takes **1 more**, for 13.
3. The dealer takes a **14th tile** and opens the game by making the first
   discard.

Sort your brand-new hand into suits — do this; chaos hands are for people
with better memories than you — and handle your flowers: any flower tiles
get laid face-up next to your hand and replaced with a draw from the **back
end** of the wall, {{< zh "補花" "bou faa" "patching the flower" >}}. Dealer
replaces first, then around the table. Replacement tiles can themselves be
flowers, which can cascade gloriously — a good omen, or at least a good bit.

## Pre-flight checklist

- Everyone has 13 tiles. The dealer has 14, briefly, before the first discard.
- Flowers are face-up on the side and replaced.
- The wall's tail end — where kong replacements and flower patches come from
  — is intact and nobody's elbow is threatening it. Remember the tail
  matters twice: the **last 14 tiles of the wall are dead** and never get
  drawn; when the live wall runs dry, the hand is over.
- Snacks and drinks are poured and within reach. We encourage both — just
  get them before the hand starts, not in the middle of one.
- Someone has already said "okay okay okay let's GO" at least twice.
